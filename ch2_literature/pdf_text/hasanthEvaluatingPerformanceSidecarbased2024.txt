Evaluating the Performance of Sidecar-based and
Sidecarless Cloud-native Service Mesh Solutions
Karumuri Meher Hasanth and Deepak Nadig
Dept. of Computer and Information Technology, Purdue University, West Lafayette, IN 47907
Email: {kmeherha, nadig}@purdue.edu
Abstract—The software development landscape is rapidly
evolving, marked by the shift from monolithic applications to mi-
croservices architectures and the adoption of agile methodologies.
While developing microservices has become more effortless, the
challenges of maintaining and deploying them persist, specifically
with managing the complex dependencies associated with mul-
tiple microservice deployments. With an increasing demand for
container orchestrators and Kubernetes emerging as the industry
standard, networking microservices remains a key challenge.
Service mesh, a solution to this problem, consists of a control
and a data plane, with the former responsible for managing the
components of the latter. Data planes in most service meshes
are implemented using sidecar proxies, posing specific challenges
when injected into application pods. A potential solution to
these challenges lies in the sidecarless approach. This paper
aims to explore the eBPF-based service mesh, Cilium, which
eliminates the need for a sidecar while encompassing most service
mesh capabilities. Further, we benchmark Cilium’s performance,
analyze its effectiveness, and compare its performance with Istio
and Linkerd. Our evaluations show that Linkerd outperforms
other solutions with respect to response times, with Linkerd
performing 29.85% and 63.43% better than Cilium and Istio,
respectively.
Index Terms—Service Mesh, Cilium, eBPF, Sidecarless.
I. INTRODUCTION
Before the rise of microservices, legacy software often
relied on monolithic architectures, impeding the transition
to agile development methods. Microservices emerged as a
solution, comprising small, independent units of code that
execute specific business functions and interact through APIs.
This migration from monolithic to microservices allowed for
the segmentation of applications into manageable compo-
nents, which aligns with agile principles. The adoption of
microservices has been further facilitated by containerization
and orchestration tools, with Kubernetes standing out as the
primary solution for effective container management.
The advent of microservices has increased the complexity of
traditional debugging methods and led to a growing need for
advanced functionalities like service discovery, load balancing,
and security. Network service mesh (NSM) addresses these
requirements by separating networking logic from applications
using proxies. Various open-source service mesh solutions
have emerged to cater to different use cases. For example, Is-
tio [1] utilizes Envoy as a sidecar proxy, providing encryption
and distributed tracing features without additional software
agents. Built with Rust, Linkerd [2] focuses on minimizing
resource consumption compared to Istio. Consul [3] stands
out for its robust service discovery capabilities and ability
to function within and outside Kubernetes. Traefik Mesh [4]
targets efficient load balancing. Although these service meshes
employ distinct strategies, they all share a common principle:
offloading networking responsibilities to a sidecar proxy. How-
ever, Cilium [5] takes a different approach by using extended
Berkeley packet filters (eBPF), eliminating the requirement for
a sidecar proxy. Nevertheless, architectures reliant on service
meshes and sidecars in service mesh solutions can increase
resource usage, potentially impacting application performance.
Further, the rapid adoption of microservices architecture,
fueled by cloud computing, has led to extensive efforts fo-
cusing on decomposing large monolithic applications into
smaller, more manageable microservices. Studies comparing
microservices to monoliths have revealed challenges such as
managing interactions, dealing with inter-service communica-
tion overhead, and addressing data management issues. The
distributed nature of microservices has introduced problems
like network failures, service discovery, load balancing, and
distributed tracing. Additionally, there are concerns about
security, initial development overheads, monitoring, and ad-
vanced traffic management for inter-service communication.
In this paper, we evaluate the benefits of sidecarless service
meshes such as Cilium. We study Cilium’s architecture and its
utilization of eBPF to eliminate the need for a sidecar. Through
this analysis, we aim to comprehensively understand Cilium
as both a service mesh and a CNI. Thus, this paper provides
valuable insights into how Cilium operates as a container net-
work interface (CNI) and a service mesh solution. To evaluate
its capabilities, we deploy a microservices application [6] that
employs the Cilium service mesh and assess its performance
using an L7 traffic generator. Furthermore, we conduct a
comparative analysis of its performance against two prominent
service mesh solutions, namely Istio and Linkerd.
This paper is organized as follows: In Section II, we intro-
duce the network service mesh, its features, and capabilities
and outline the related works. In Section III, we detail Cilium’s
internal architecture and its operation. Section IV presents a
qualitative comparison of Istio, Linkerd, and Cilium. In Sec-
tion V, we describe our evaluation framework and experimental
setup. In Section VI, we present a comprehensive performance
analysis of three service solutions and discuss their capabilities
and limitations. Further, we present the suitability of each
service mesh solution for specific use cases in terms of its
performance, ease of use, resource, and operational costs.

II. BACKGROUND AND RELATED WORK
This section provides a brief background on network service
meshes, their features and capabilities, and the related works
in the context of benchmarking service mesh implementations.
A. Network Service Mesh (NSM)
A network service mesh (NSM), or service mesh, is an
infrastructure layer designed to manage service-to-service
communication within microservices architectures, focusing
on secure, reliable, and observable interactions. It provides
functionalities such as service discovery, load balancing, en-
cryption, authorization, and observability without modifying
the application code. This paper will examine three primary
service mesh solutions, namely Istio, Linkerd, and Cilium.
Istio and Linkerd will serve as key benchmarks, as these
two solutions are adopted by 88% of the industry service
mesh deployments based on market share data [7], with Istio
and Linkerd accounting for 47% and 41% of production use,
respectively.
B. Service Mesh Capabilities and Features
The key capabilities and features of service meshes include:
1) Traffic Management: Traffic management is a funda-
mental feature of a service mesh that provides load balancing,
traffic routing, and traffic shifting capabilities. Load balancing
ensures that incoming requests are evenly distributed among
the available service instances, optimizing resource utilization
and preventing overloads. Traffic routing allows for intelligent
routing based on various criteria like service names, paths, and
headers, enabling flexible traffic management. Traffic shifting
is essential for controlled deployments, enabling canary re-
leases and gradual rollouts of new versions to minimize the
impact of potential issues on the entire system.
2) Service Discovery: Service discovery simplifies man-
aging dynamically changing service infrastructures. Dynamic
service registration automatically registers new services as
they come online and removes them when they go offline.
A service catalog maintains an up-to-date list of available
services and their locations, simplifying service lookup. DNS
resolution further aids service discovery by providing a con-
venient way for applications to locate services.
3) Observability: Observability features in a service mesh
provide insights into the performance and behavior of mi-
croservices. Metrics collection records data on request/re-
sponse times, error rates, and other performance indicators.
Distributed tracing offers visibility into the flow of requests
across multiple microservices, aiding in debugging and per-
formance analysis. Centralized logging aggregates logs from
all services, simplifying troubleshooting and auditing.
4) Traffic Telemetry: Traffic telemetry involves monitoring
the health of services and collecting performance data. Service
health checks periodically assess the health of services and
divert traffic away from unhealthy instances. Metrics and
analytics offer insights into the system’s overall performance
and health. Alerting capabilities provide notifications and
alarms for detecting and responding to abnormal behavior or
performance degradation.
5) A/B Testing and Canary Releases: A/B testing and
Canary releases allow controlled testing of new features or
versions. In a service mesh, traffic can be selectively directed
to a test group, enabling the evaluation of changes or new ver-
sions in a production-like environment before full deployment,
thus reducing risks and ensuring a smoother transition.
6) Multi-Cluster and Multi-Cloud Support: Service meshes
support
communication
between
microservices
deployed
across different clusters or cloud providers. This capability
is essential for hybrid and multi-cloud deployments, where
services may span various environments.
7) Policy Enforcement: Policy enforcement [8] in a service
mesh involves defining and enforcing rules that govern the
behavior of microservices, ensuring security, traffic manage-
ment, access control, and compliance. This includes enforcing
authentication and authorization, encrypting communications,
managing traffic through rate limiting and retries, and imple-
menting role-based access controls. Additionally, it ensures
compliance through auditing, data residency, and operational
policies like circuit breakers and load balancing. These policies
are centrally managed by the control plane and enforced at
runtime by the data plane, ensuring consistent and reliable
service operations across the microservices architecture.
C. Related Work
The industry has rapidly embraced the microservices model,
particularly with cloud computing facilitating faster adoption.
The work by the authors in [9] shows how to systematically
break down monolithic applications into small, independent
microservices, establishing functional boundaries. The au-
thors in [10] present a comparison of the performance of
microservices and monoliths across different granularities,
analyzing metrics such as CPU performance, memory usage,
disk consumption, network performance, response time, and
bandwidth estimation. Microservices can increase complexity,
especially in managing interactions, ensuring data consistency,
and coordinating deployments [11]. Inter-service communica-
tion overhead, typically through APIs or network calls, leads to
increased latency and potential bottlenecks [12]. Data manage-
ment becomes more complex, requiring system-level support
beyond software engineering practices [13]. The distributed
nature of microservices introduces issues like network failures,
service discovery, and load balancing [14]. Security challenges
have also been overlooked, with existing research showing
gaps in addressing vulnerabilities in microservices architec-
ture [15]. Additionally, the initial development overhead for
microservices can delay time-to-market [16], and monitoring
and observability become increasingly difficult as the number

III. CILIUM ARCHITECTURE AND EBPF
Cilium leverages eBPF to provide visibility and control over
systems and applications, achieving granularity and efficiency
typical of in-kernel observability techniques. These enhance-
ments are implemented seamlessly without necessitating any
changes to the application code. eBPF works effectively with
modern containerized workloads and more traditional setups
like virtual machines and standard Linux processes. However,
this approach introduces challenges and opportunities in se-
curing service-to-service communications. Traditional Linux
network security methods, such as iptables, rely on IP
addresses and TCP/UDP ports for filtering traffic, but these
IP addresses often change in dynamic microservices environ-
ments. The transient nature of containers makes traditional
iptables methods less effective in adapting to application
deployments, resulting in scaling issues with access control
lists (ACL) and load-balancing tables. Additionally, using
protocol ports as a means of security differentiation is no
longer practical, as these ports are used for a wide range of
service communications in container orchestration systems.
Fig. 1: Cilium Deployment Architecture
Cilium addresses these challenges using eBPF to provide
security, visibility, and policy enforcement based on service,
pod, or container identity instead of IP addresses. This ap-
proach allows filtering at the application layer, simplifying the
application of security policy in highly dynamic environments
by decoupling security from addressing. Further, it enhances
security isolation by operating at the HTTP layer rather than
traditional Layer 3 and Layer 4 segmentation.
Cilium integrates the service mesh layer directly into the
kernel via eBPF, eliminating the need for sidecar proxies. This
innovative approach effectively manages connectivity at both
the networking and application protocol layers, handling pro-
tocols like IP, TCP, UDP, HTTP, Kafka, gRPC, and DNS with
high efficiency. By utilizing eBPF, Cilium offers fast, scalable
networking that overcomes the performance limitations typi-
cally associated with traditional proxies, enabling direct and
efficient communication between cloud-native services.
Additionally, Cilium provides a range of control plane
options, allowing users to balance complexity and features
according to their needs. These options include more straight-
forward choices like Ingress and Gateway API, as well as more
comprehensive solutions like Istio and the full capabilities of
Envoy accessed through the Envoy custom resource definition
(CRD). Figure 1 shows a typical Cilium deployment with its
internal architecture. The Cilium agent runs on each worker
node and interacts with the deployed microservices through the
agent with minimal overheads. The main components of the
Cilium service mesh include Cilium components and Hubble,
as detailed next.
A. Cilium Components
The following components for the core of the Cilium service
mesh infrastructure.
1) Agent: The Cilium agent cilium-agent operates on
every cluster node. Its core function is to receive configuration
directives via Kubernetes or APIs, covering aspects such as
network, service, and policy management, meeting monitoring
requirements, and load-balancing workloads. The agent tracks
container orchestration events from Kubernetes to identify
container and workload states. The Cilium agent is responsible
for managing eBPF programs in the kernel, which control
container ingress and egress traffic.
2) CLI: The Cilium CLI client (cilium) is a command-
line tool installed alongside the Cilium agent. It communicates
with the REST API of the Cilium agent on the same node.
The CLI enables users to inspect the state and status of the
local agent and offers tools to access and validate the state of
eBPF maps directly.
3) Operator: The Cilium operator is designed to manage
cluster-wide tasks in a Kubernetes environment, centralizing
functions that only need to be performed once for the entire
cluster rather than on each node. This includes responsibilities
like IP Address Management (IPAM) and maintaining state
information in a key-value store (kvstore). The operator
handles these tasks centrally and ensures consistency and
efficiency across the cluster. Notably, the operator is not part
of the critical path for real-time network traffic forwarding
or policy enforcement. This allows the cluster to continue
operating normally even if the operator is temporarily unavail-
able. However, the unavailability of the Cilium Operator can
lead to specific issues depending on the cluster configuration.
If the operator is required to allocate new IP addresses,
its downtime can delay scheduling new workloads due to
delays in IPAM. Additionally, the operator’s role in updating
the kvstore heartbeat key is crucial for maintaining the
perceived health of the kvstore. If this update fails, Cilium
agents might declare the kvstore unhealthy and restart,
causing potential disruptions. Therefore, while not critical for
immediate network operations, the operator’s availability is
essential for smooth and timely cluster management.

B. Hubble
Cilium uses Hubble for observability, and the following
outlines how Hubble interacts with Cilium.
1) Server: The Hubble server operates on each node, col-
lecting eBPF-based visibility data from Cilium. It is integrated
into the Cilium agent to ensure high performance and minimal
overhead. The server provides a gRPC service for retrieving
flows and Prometheus metrics.
2) Relay:
Relay (hubble-relay) is an independent
component that recognizes all active Hubble servers and
provides visibility into the cluster using the corresponding
gRPC APIs. It provides a unified API that represents all the
servers within the cluster.
3) CLI and GUI:

hubble-relay or the local server to retrieve flow events.
The graphical user interface (Hubble-UI) leverages relay-based
visibility to provide a graphical representation (See Figure 2)
of service dependencies and application connectivity within
the cluster.

corporates a sidecar-less architecture, simplifying deployment.
Key features of Cilium include service discovery, load balanc-
ing, traffic management, security, observability, policy enforce-
ment, rate limiting, canary rollouts, and support for various
protocols. Table I presents a comparative analysis of these

grows. In contrast to Istio and Linkerd, Cilium takes a unique

V. EXPERIMENTAL SETUP
Our experimental setup relies on high-performance bare
metal nodes on the Chameleon Cloud testbed [19]. We deploy
a Kubernetes cluster on bare metal nodes with dual-socket
AMD EPYC 7763 processors with 128 cores, 256 threads,
and 256 GiB of memory. A three-node cluster, including one
master and two worker nodes, is deployed with Kubernetes
version 1.24. We also deploy a complete microservices ap-
plication (Google online botique [6]) comprising eleven inter-
connected microservices. We expose the application frontend
through a NodePort configuration and a public IP. We employ
Locust [20] to generate traffic on several microservice appli-
cation endpoints, including HTTP GET and POST requests, to
evaluate the service meshes’ robustness under various usage
scenarios. Istio version v1.22.3 was deployed, followed by
Linkerd v2.16, which employed the Calico v3.28 CNI. Lastly,
Cilium v1.16.1 (also a CNI) was deployed, and the micro-
services were meshed using the service mesh infrastructure.

the microservices’ endpoints. Locust [20] is an open-source

scalable, with Python scripting capabilities for testing various
web applications. Locust supports distributed testing, allowing
us to simulate millions of simultaneous users with multiple
machines. Further, it offers the flexibility to create complex test
scenarios. Locust also provides various performance metrics,
such as the number of requests per second, response times, and
failure rates, aiding in analyzing application performance and

B. Latency Analysis
Figure 4a and Figure 4b illustrate the request completion
time percentiles for GET and POST endpoints, respectively.
From the figures, we note that 99% of requests for the Cilium
service mesh were completed, on average, within 160 ms for
the GET requests and 200 ms for the POST requests. This data
was collected on the /GET CART endpoint deployed on the
BareMetal node. We used the Cilium CNI for this evaluation,
and we note that the response times increase exponentially
from the 50th percentile to the 99th percentile. Further, we
note that a few requests take about thrice the 99th percentile to
complete for GET requests and about twice the 99th percentile
for POST requests.
C. Response Time Comparison
Figure 4c illustrates the performance of three primary
service mesh solutions under identical workloads and system
conditions. We employed Locust to generate L7 application
traffic and generate real-time load. Compared to Istio and
Linkerd, Cilium consistently outperforms Istio with lower
response time latencies, as shown in Figure 4c. Although Link-
ered performs better in our tests, it lacks critical service mesh
capabilities and is less usable than the other two solutions
(See Section VI-D). Istio uses Envoy proxy as a sidecar and
provides rich metrics with the highest adoption in production.
Linkerd uses a lightweight rust-based proxy as a sidecar, and
Cilium takes a unique sidecarless route. We measured the
response time latencies for each service mesh for 25 users
per test at a spawn rate of 2 users per second and a 10-
minute runtime. We observe that Linkerd outperforms Istio and
Cilium with consistently low response times, indicating better
performance. Further, Istio has the highest response times of
the three solutions. Cilium performs better than Istio in each
case, with a response time higher than Linkerd.
D. Discussion
Our performance analysis shows that Linkerd is the best-
performing service mesh among the three solutions. However,
the suitability of a particular service mesh solution depends
on additional factors, including CNI choice, resource over-
heads, and operational costs. A key consideration that impacts
resource overheads and operations/maintenance costs is the
choice of CNI. Linkerd and Istio cannot function indepen-
dently without a CNI. While both support most mainstream
CNIs, CNI deployments and maintenance represent an addi-
tional burden on the operator and the infrastructure. Cilium,
on the other hand, serves as both CNI and service mesh.
This simplifies deployments and maintenance and reduces
overall resource utilization and operational overheads. As
the CNI consumes valuable cluster resources in the form of
DaemonSets and Deployments, Cilium eases administra-
tive burdens as the operator no longer manages two different
control plane components. Table II compares various factors

VII. CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK
In this paper, we present a comprehensive performance anal-
ysis of three service mesh solutions, including Istio, Linkerd,
and Cilium. We present a comprehensive qualitative analysis
of their features and capabilities. Our testbed evaluations show
that Linkerd outperforms the other two with the lowest average
response times. We note that Linkerd performs 29.85% better
than Cilium and 63.43% better than Istio. With its unique
eBPF-based architecture, Cilium performs better than Istio and
is a better choice for reducing overall resource utilization and
operational costs cluster-wide. Further, as Cilium is both a CNI
and a service mesh, its reduced resource requirements may
suit edge devices where deploying a full-fledged service mesh
is infeasible. With the absence of a sidecar, Cilium is also a
suitable solution that can be offloaded to hardware accelerators
such as DPU [21]. Our future work will focus on evaluating
the impact of various CNIs on service mesh performance.
